Understanding Dementia

Beyond Alzheimer's

"Dementia is not a disease — it's a syndrome. Think of it like fever: fever isn't a disease, it's a sign that something is wrong. Dementia is the same — a sign that the brain is being affected by an underlying condition."

Alzheimer's Disease (60–80% of cases): Gradual memory loss starting with recent events. Amyloid plaques and tau tangles damage brain cells. Earliest sign is often repeating questions or forgetting recent conversations.
Lewy Body Dementia: Visual hallucinations, movement problems similar to Parkinson's, fluctuating alertness, and swallowing difficulties. Often misdiagnosed. Sleep disturbances (acting out dreams) are common early signs.
Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD): Personality and behavior changes come first — not memory loss. May involve loss of empathy, impulsive behavior, or language difficulties. Often affects people under 65.
Vascular Dementia: Caused by reduced blood flow to the brain (often after strokes). Thinking and planning are affected more than memory. Step-wise decline rather than gradual progression.
  • Memory loss that disrupts daily life.
  • Challenges in planning or solving problems.
  • Difficulty completing familiar tasks.
  • Confusion with time or place.
  • Trouble understanding visual images.
  • New problems with words in speaking or writing.
  • Misplacing things and losing the ability to retrace steps.
  • Decreased or poor judgment.
  • Withdrawal from work or social activities.
  • Changes in mood and personality.
Every person progresses differently — stages are a general roadmap, not a fixed timeline. Some people stay in one stage for years.
Early stage (mild): Still mostly independent. Forgetting recent events, misplacing things, mild personality changes. This is the time to plan — legally, financially, and socially.
Middle stage (moderate): Needs help with daily activities. Confusion about time and place, personality changes, wandering risk. Often the longest stage.
Late stage (severe): Needs full-time care. Loss of speech, mobility issues, swallowing difficulties. The focus shifts to comfort and dignity.
Getting an accurate diagnosis involves a combination of medical history, cognitive exams, physical checks, and sometimes brain imaging. Seeking early diagnosis is vital — getting answers early provides access to specific treatments, clinical trials, and maximum time to plan for the future financially and legally.
Caregiving is incredibly demanding both emotionally and physically. It is absolutely crucial to assemble a Village of support. Build your care team early, tap into local adult day centers or respite care, and never hesitate to ask for help. "Sainthood is not required" — you are allowed to have bad days.
Following a diagnosis, living well involves prioritizing physical health, staying spiritually and socially active, and exploring current treatment options. Focus equally on maintaining quality of life through hobbies, routine schedules, and managing stress levels actively.
Content synthesized from the Alzheimer's Association (alz.org) and clinician-reviewed care guidance.

Beyond Alzheimer's

Not every dementia looks the same

Most caregivers walk in expecting Alzheimer's because that's what they've heard of. But about 1 in 3 dementia diagnoses are something else — and the care strategies are different. If what you're seeing doesn't quite match the Alzheimer's picture, read on.

“He had started acting very differently … it was kind of demeaning … I thought he had turned into a big jerk. We had been in marriage counseling, and I was actually 2 months away from leaving.” — A spouse, before diagnosis
What it is: FTD damages the frontal and temporal lobes — the parts of the brain that govern personality, judgment, empathy, and language. Memory may stay intact for years. There are two main forms: behavioural-variant (bvFTD) and language-variant (primary progressive aphasia).
What's different from Alzheimer's:
  • Personality change comes first, not memory loss. Coldness, rudeness, loss of empathy, impulsive spending, inappropriate jokes, changes in food preferences.
  • Younger onset — often diagnosed between 45 and 65. Families often spend years thinking it's depression, midlife crisis, or marriage trouble.
  • They often don't know anything is wrong (loss of insight). Don't expect them to agree they've changed.
Practical care:
  • Protect money early — impulsive spending and scam vulnerability appear before anyone suspects dementia.
  • Avoid reasoning, debating, or lecturing — the empathy circuits that would make those work are damaged.
  • Use structure and routine to reduce the social situations where harm happens.
  • Connect with the Association for Frontotemporal Degeneration (theaftd.org) — the support community looks different from the Alzheimer's one.
For the caregiver: If you've spent years thinking your spouse, sibling, or parent had simply become unkind — a diagnosis can be its own kind of grief and its own kind of relief. Forgiveness, including of yourself, is part of the work.
“He had a really bad hallucination, where I couldn't get him out of it. And he kind of grabbed me. He wasn't hurting me, but my daughter came out, and she saw it.” — A spouse
What it is: Abnormal protein deposits (Lewy bodies) build up in brain cells. LBD overlaps with Parkinson's disease — some patients are diagnosed with Parkinson's first.
What's different from Alzheimer's:
  • Vivid visual hallucinations — often early, often people or animals that aren't there. Usually not scary to them; can be very scary to caregivers.
  • Fluctuating alertness — sharp one hour, confused the next. This is not them “trying.”
  • Movement problems — slowness, shuffling gait, falls, stiff face, soft voice. Looks like Parkinson's.
  • REM sleep behaviour disorder — acting out dreams (kicking, shouting) often years before diagnosis. Worth mentioning to the neurologist.
  • Severe sensitivity to typical antipsychotics — haloperidol and similar can cause dangerous reactions. Always tell every prescriber and every ER that the diagnosis is LBD.
  • Swallowing difficulties often appear earlier than in Alzheimer's — watch for coughing on liquids.
Practical care:
  • For hallucinations: don't argue, don't agree, redirect — “I see you're upset. Let's go in the other room and have some water.”
  • Remove fall hazards aggressively — rugs, loose cords, dim hallways.
  • Carry a wallet card listing the LBD diagnosis and the antipsychotic warning for ER visits.
  • Lewy Body Dementia Association: lbda.org — their hotline is excellent.
If you see this today: sudden severe confusion change, new face droop or arm weakness, sudden fall — call 911. LBD increases stroke and infection vulnerability.
“He was fine and then he wasn't. And then he was fine again, but a little worse than before. Every few months it would step down.” — A family caregiver
What it is: Reduced blood flow to the brain — often from strokes (large or many tiny ones), high blood pressure, or diabetes — damages thinking and planning more than memory at first.
What's different from Alzheimer's:
  • Step-wise decline instead of smooth, gradual loss. Long plateaus then sudden drops, often after a small stroke (TIA).
  • Executive function goes first — planning a meal, following directions, paying bills — while memory of facts may be relatively spared.
  • Slower thinking, less initiative. They may seem “there” but take much longer to respond.
  • Mood changes — depression and apathy are common.
Practical care:
  • Manage the vascular risk factors aggressively — blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol, smoking. Slowing further damage is one of the few things you can directly do.
  • Give them more time to answer rather than jumping in. They may know the answer; the retrieval is slow.
  • Break tasks into one step at a time.
  • Watch for signs of a new stroke (face droop, arm weakness, slurred speech — FAST) and call 911 immediately.
Mixed dementia is more common than people realize, especially in people over 80 — usually a combination of Alzheimer's plus vascular changes, or Alzheimer's plus Lewy body. The care plan blends approaches.
Parkinson's disease dementia develops in some people with Parkinson's a year or more after movement symptoms appear. Care is similar to Lewy body care.
Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) — the classic triad is trouble walking, urinary incontinence, and dementia. NPH is sometimes treatable with a shunt; worth asking a neurologist about if these three appear together.
Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD), alcohol-related dementia, HIV-associated dementia, and others exist but are less common. If the decline is unusually fast (weeks to months, not years), push hard for a specialist work-up.
Always ask your clinician: “What is the most likely type of dementia, and what is the second most likely?” Many community physicians default to “Alzheimer's” without specifying. The type matters for care planning, medication safety, and prognosis.

NIH Caregiver Guide

Page-cited deep dives

Drawn from the National Institute on Aging's free Caring for a Person with Alzheimer's Disease guide (NIH Publication No. 23-AG-8040, public domain). Page citations let you trace every claim back to the source.

“People with Alzheimer's also may experience sundowning — restlessness, agitation, irritability, or confusion that can begin or worsen as daylight begins to fade. Being overly tired can increase late-afternoon and nighttime restlessness.” — NIH Caregiver Guide, p. 17
Key points:
  • Sleep is often inverted: too much, too little, or repeated waking (p. 17)
  • Sundowning timing is biological — tied to the brain's internal clock (p. 17)
  • Fatigue compounds it; an over-tired person sundowns harder (p. 17)
  • Daytime exercise and morning light are the strongest non-drug levers (pp. 17, 26)
  • Alcohol and caffeine reliably worsen it (p. 17)
Practical tips:
  • Schedule the largest meal and any bathing in the middle of the day
  • Limit long naps and dozing after 3 PM
  • Build a calming evening ritual — low lights, soft music, reading aloud
  • Same bedtime nightly; nightlights in bedroom, hall, bathroom
  • Cut caffeine after lunch and alcohol entirely
Call the doctor: Sudden onset or rapid worsening of sleep disruption — especially after an infection or medication change (p. 12).
“My mom was a very strong-willed lady. She absolutely hated the shower — even once a week was difficult… I just didn't want her to get hurt, so I was trying to be there for her safety.” — Caregiver quoted in NIH Guide, p. 22
Key points:
  • Resistance usually comes from fear, embarrassment, or physical discomfort (p. 21)
  • Two or three baths a week is enough; sponge baths fill the gaps (p. 23)
  • Choices preserve dignity ("bath now or in 15 minutes?") (p. 21)
  • Handheld showerhead starting at the feet is less threatening than the face (p. 22)
  • Never leave a person with Alzheimer's alone in tub or shower (p. 22)
Practical tips:
  • Gather every supply before you start — never leave the room
  • Water heater at 120°F; rubber bathmat, grab bars, bath chair
  • Drape a towel over shoulders/lap and wash under it for modesty
  • Narrate every step: "I'm going to wash your arm now"
  • If a full bath fails, switch to a seated wash-up at the sink
Call the doctor: Any unexplained rash, skin breakdown, or fall during bathing. Ask for a home OT evaluation if transfers feel unsafe (pp. 22, 47).
“When someone with Alzheimer's is not able to think clearly and make good decisions quickly, they should stop driving. If the person keeps driving when it is no longer safe, someone could get hurt or be killed.” — NIH Caregiver Guide, p. 35
Red flags:
  • New dents or scratches; takes a long time on simple errands; comes home and can't explain why (p. 35)
  • Two or more tickets or minor accidents (p. 35)
  • Comments from neighbours about driving (p. 35)
  • Speeding or sudden lane changes (p. 35)
  • Confusing the brake and gas pedals (p. 35)
Practical tips:
  • Request a DMV driving evaluation or older-adult risk screening
  • Ask the doctor to write "Do not drive" on a prescription pad — often the key turn-around moment
  • If refusal continues: hide keys, disable the car, move it out of sight, or sell
  • Replace driving with rides: Rides in Sight (ridesinsight.org / 855-607-4337)
  • Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov / 800-677-1116) for senior transport
Act fast: As soon as red flags appear — earlier than feels comfortable (p. 35).
“Having meaningful conversations with the person living with Alzheimer's to learn about their values and preferences is the most important part of advance care planning.” — NIH Caregiver Guide, p. 6
Six core documents (p. 7):
  • Durable POA for Finances — legal & financial decisions
  • Durable POA for Health Care — names a health-care proxy
  • Living Will — instructs doctors on treatment wishes
  • Do Not Resuscitate form if appropriate
  • Will — distribution of property after death
  • Living Trust — optional, depending on estate complexity
Practical tips:
  • Store all documents together in a fireproof lockbox
  • Have the values conversation FIRST; paperwork second
  • Set up automated bill-pay for utilities and mortgage to avoid lapses
  • Add their phone number to donotcall.gov; enable bank fraud alerts
  • Early-onset Alzheimer's qualifies for SSDI "compassionate allowances" — faster approval (p. 56)
“In the later stages of Alzheimer's, the person may no longer be able to chew and swallow easily, which increases their risk of choking. If the person chokes on a bite of food, there is a chance that the food could go into the lungs. This can cause pneumonia, which can lead to death.” — NIH Caregiver Guide, p. 60
Key points:
  • Aspiration pneumonia is a leading cause of death in late-stage Alzheimer's (p. 60)
  • Appetite naturally declines late; over-feeding/force-feeding does harm (p. 26)
  • Pocketed food in the cheeks is a common, missed risk (p. 60)
  • Thickened liquids move down more slowly — reduce aspiration (p. 61)
  • Sitting upright 20+ minutes after the meal matters as much as during (p. 61)
Practical tips:
  • Cut food small; offer soft textures — yogurt, applesauce, mashed avocado, banana
  • Use a thickener (xanthan gum, corn starch, maltodextrin) in thin drinks
  • Skip straws — small sips from a cup are safer
  • Say the word "swallow" as a verbal cue between bites
  • Sit BESIDE the person, not in front — less intimidating
Call the doctor: Any coughing during meals, wet/gurgly voice after drinking, unexplained fevers, or weight loss — request a swallow study (p. 61).
“Once a person stops walking or stays in one position too long, they often develop sores or other skin issues. Skin problems can be extremely uncomfortable for someone in the last stages of life.” — NIH Caregiver Guide, p. 61
Key points:
  • Pressure sores form fastest on heels, hips, lower back, back of head — check these daily (p. 62)
  • First sign is discoloured or darkened skin — not an open wound (p. 62)
  • Reposition every 2 hours sitting, every 1 hour lying (p. 62)
  • Foam/gel/air/water pads on the mattress reduce pressure (p. 62)
  • Catching at the "redness" stage prevents serious breakdown — call early (p. 62)
Practical tips:
  • Set a phone alarm every 1–2 hours for repositioning
  • Daily skin check at bath time — heels, sacrum, behind ears, back of head
  • Massage with unscented lotion for circulation and comfort
  • Use wedge cushions to relieve pressure on bony prominences
  • Keep skin dry between folds; treat any rash early
Call the doctor: First sign of dark or discoloured skin over a bony area — don't wait for an open wound (p. 62).
“A trip to the emergency room can be very stressful for both the person with Alzheimer's and you.” — NIH Caregiver Guide, p. 30
Key points:
  • Hospital environments accelerate confusion — preparation buffers it (p. 30)
  • Staff must be told the person has dementia explicitly — assume nothing (p. 30)
  • One person handles paperwork while another stays bedside (p. 30)
  • Overnight stays should have a family/friend present (p. 30)
  • Carry medication and advance-directive documents on you — emergencies don't schedule (p. 30)
Practical tips:
  • Keep a printed "hospital go-bag" ready: meds list, allergies, insurance cards, advance directives, primary doctor contact
  • Add a comfort item — photo, blanket, favourite snack
  • Repeat the dementia diagnosis to EACH new staff member
  • For non-emergency visits: book her best time of day, off-peak hours
  • Ask about telehealth for routine follow-ups (p. 30)
Go to the ER: Sudden behaviour change after a recent illness, medication change, or fall — don't wait (pp. 12, 28).
“It's extremely nerve-wracking trying to figure out how you're going to pay for care every month.” — Caregiver quoted in NIH Guide, p. 54
Key points:
  • Most families pay from a patchwork — personal funds, government, private financing (p. 54)
  • Medicare does NOT pay for assisted living or long-term custodial care (p. 55)
  • Medicaid does cover long-term care for those who qualify; rules vary by state (p. 55)
  • PACE (Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly) helps eligible people stay home (p. 55)
  • Many states pay a family member as the caregiver under Medicaid waivers (p. 57)
Make these calls:
  • Eldercare Locator 800-677-1116 — the single best starting point
  • SHIP at 877-839-2675 — free Medicaid/Medicare guidance, one-on-one
  • benefitscheckup.org — free, anonymous, finds programs you may not know exist
  • caregiver.va.gov / 855-260-3274 if a veteran or veteran's spouse
  • Ask your state Medicaid agency about home and community-based services waivers

Source: National Institute on Aging. Caring for a Person with Alzheimer's Disease: Your Easy-to-Use Guide. NIH Publication No. 23-AG-8040, December 2023. Public domain. Full PDF: nia.nih.gov/health/caregiving.

🛡️ Safety Guides

Warning signs: Coughing or choking during/after eating, wet or gurgling voice after swallowing, food pooling in cheeks, drooling, weight loss.
What helps:
  • Sit upright at 90° during meals and for 30 minutes after.
  • Remind them to swallow between bites: “Stop and swallow.”
  • Offer softer textures; thicken liquids if recommended by a speech therapist.
  • Small bites, slow pace — never rush meals.
  • Keep medications locked away; dispense one at a time.
  • Request a formal swallowing assessment from a Speech-Language Pathologist.
Source: alz.org clinical guidelines and family caregiver experience.
Who should know: Close family, trusted neighbors (especially if wandering risk), bank tellers (to watch for unusual withdrawals), the primary care doctor, a few trusted friends.
Who may not need to know: Casual acquaintances, distant relatives who might gossip, anyone with a financial interest in the person.
Protecting against scams:
  • Freeze credit reports at all three bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion).
  • Set daily spending caps on debit cards.
  • Block unknown phone numbers; register on the Do Not Call list.
  • Set up online banking portals so you can monitor activity.
  • Watch for “caregiver resource” sites that are actually lead-generation brokers — they sell your information.
Source: elder-care guidance and family caregiver experience.

Help & Tips

Simple guides and helpful resources

• Open blinds to let in natural light as soon as you wake up.
• Keep a steady routine: wash, get dressed, eat breakfast around the same time.
• Place items (toothbrush, clothes) in order of use.
• Eat colorful fruits, veggies, and fiber-rich foods daily.
• Drink plenty of water throughout the day to stay refreshed.
• Snack on nuts, yogurt, or fresh fruit when you feel peckish.
• A gentle walk outside is great for both body and mind.
• Try light stretching or chair exercises in the morning.
• Gardening or folding laundry are excellent ways to stay active.
• Sit upright at 90° during meals and for 30 minutes after.
• Take small bites, chew slowly, and swallow completely before taking another bite.
• Thicken liquids or choose soft foods if recommended by your therapist.
• Never give your bank card, money, or passwords to strangers on the phone.
• Block numbers you do not know, and register for scam call filters.
• Tell your caregiver immediately if you receive suspicious letters or emails.
