Understanding Alzheimer's & Dementia

Educational Hub

"Alzheimer's is the most common form of dementia. It causes problems with memory, thinking and behavior."

Alzheimer's is a specific brain disease that progressively destroys memory and thinking skills. It is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for 60-80% of cases. Over time, symptoms grow severe enough to interfere with daily tasks.
  • Memory loss that disrupts daily life.
  • Challenges in planning or solving problems.
  • Difficulty completing familiar tasks.
  • Confusion with time or place.
  • Trouble understanding visual images.
  • New problems with words in speaking or writing.
  • Misplacing things and losing the ability to retrace steps.
  • Decreased or poor judgment.
  • Withdrawal from work or social activities.
  • Changes in mood and personality.
Getting an accurate diagnosis involves a combination of medical history, cognitive exams, physical checks, and sometimes brain imaging. Seeking early diagnosis is vital—getting answers early provides access to specific treatments, clinical trials, and maximum time to plan for the future financially and legally.
Alzheimer's disease typically progresses in three general stages: Early-stage (mild), Middle-stage (moderate), and Late-stage (severe). In the early stage, individuals can often still function independently but notice memory lapses. By the late stage, extensive care is required as people lose the ability to respond to their environment.
Caregiving is incredibly demanding both emotionally and physically. It is absolutely crucial to assemble a Village of support. Build your care team early, tap into local adult day centers or respite care, and never hesitate to ask for help from friends and neighbors to avoid severe caregiver burnout.
Following a diagnosis, living well involves prioritizing physical health, staying spiritually and socially active, and exploring current treatment options. Focus equally on maintaining a high quality of life through hobbies, routine schedules, and managing stress levels actively.
